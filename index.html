Chapter 1The Rise of Globalization

Lecture guide: Session1 chapter1.pptx, slides 6-20.

1.What are some underlying factors that are causing decoupling to happen as societies move from a unipolar to a multipolar world? In your opinion, in which direction is the balance of global economic power shifting? And is it good for the world economy?

Decoupling, as used in the slides, means emerging economies increasingly grow through their own economic strengths rather than relying mainly on developed economies. The main drivers are expanding middle classes and domestic demand, high savings and investment, better technical education, innovation, and stronger trade between emerging economies.

In the course framework, economic power is becoming more widely shared between established economies and emerging economies such as the BRIC countries: Brazil, Russia, India and China. I consider a multipolar economy beneficial when it creates more sources of demand, investment and innovation. However, benefits depend on cooperation and sound institutions; rivalry and trade fragmentation can reduce them. Decoupling here does not mean countries stop trading with one another.

2.What are some of the major economic reform themes that are common to the three international organizations promoting globalization?

The IMF, World Bank and WTO share broad themes of international cooperation, more open trade and investment, transparent institutions, predictable policies, and sustainable economic development. Fair competition and reduced discrimination help countries participate in global markets.

Their responsibilities differ: the IMF concentrates on monetary and financial stability, the World Bank on development and poverty reduction, and the WTO on trade rules and negotiations. These complementary roles help make globalization more stable and accessible.

3.Globalization can be facilitated only if national governments are willing to participate in that process. What roles can the three major international institutions play to be a part of this facilitation process?

  • IMF: promotes monetary cooperation, provides economic advice and technical assistance, and lends to countries facing balance-of-payments difficulties.
  • World Bank: finances development and infrastructure and supports institutional capacity, knowledge transfer and poverty reduction.
  • WTO: provides a forum for trade negotiations, administers trade agreements, reviews trade policies and supports dispute settlement and trade-related training.

These institutions provide resources, rules and coordination, but national governments must agree to reforms and implement them domestically.

4.While domestic institutions play an important role in the globalization process, what are some of the fundamental policy measures that those countries need to promote in order to benefit from globalization? How might these policies be implemented and promoted?

Countries need good governance, competitive markets, secure property rights and effective anticorruption policies. Investors and businesses need confidence that contracts will be honoured, decisions will be transparent and competitors will receive fair treatment.

Implementation includes capable and accountable civil servants, an independent judiciary, transparent procurement, consistent enforcement, and simpler business procedures. Education, infrastructure and support for displaced workers also help people benefit from new opportunities. Governments should publish clear rules, consult affected groups and measure whether reforms improve outcomes.

5.Do you believe that there is a “digital divide” in the global economy? Is globalization narrowing that gap? If so, how?

Yes. The digital divide includes differences in affordable internet access, reliable infrastructure, devices and digital skills. Globalization can narrow it by spreading technology, encouraging telecommunications investment and increasing competition.

For example, mobile networks allow communities to access online banking and education without first building extensive landline systems: this is leapfrogging. However, cheaper technology alone does not remove the divide. Rural coverage, affordability, education and accessible services remain necessary to ensure that people can use digital opportunities.

6.Is sustainable business and economic development the answer to the globalization controversy?

Sustainable development is part of the answer, but it is not sufficient alone. It combines economic growth with social well-being and environmental protection, helping address concerns about exploitation, pollution and unequal benefits.

Businesses can improve labour conditions and resource efficiency; governments can enforce standards and provide social protection; and NGOs can represent communities and monitor outcomes. Globalization becomes more acceptable when these groups work together so that gains are widely shared and the people bearing adjustment costs receive support.

7.Globalization temporarily creates “winners” and “losers.” What reform would you suggest to help those who are not benefiting from globalization? How might these reforms be implemented?

I would recommend job retraining, career guidance, job-matching services, temporary income support and access to essential health care. Regions losing major industries may also need investment in infrastructure and new businesses.

Governments can fund support through public budgets, employers can provide apprenticeships and retraining, and educational institutions and NGOs can deliver accessible programmes. Assistance should connect workers to actual vacancies and be assessed through employment and earnings outcomes. This helps people move into expanding industries while reducing the hardship caused by economic change.

Chapter 2The Evolution of International Business

Lecture guide: Session2 chapter2.pptx, slides 3-15.

8.How would you make a convincing case that open trade in goods and services as well as free flow of foreign direct investment will enhance the well-being of (a) consumers, (b) producers, and (c) the government of countries? Give specific examples to prove your position.

  • Consumers: imports increase choice and competition, which can lower prices and improve quality. For example, a household can choose between locally produced and imported appliances instead of relying on one domestic supplier.
  • Producers: firms can obtain cheaper or better inputs, reach larger markets and learn from foreign investors. For example, a local manufacturer supplying a foreign-owned factory may gain technical knowledge and larger orders.
  • Government: additional investment, employment and business activity can expand the income and corporate tax base. For example, a new foreign-owned hotel can create jobs and demand for local transport and food suppliers.

These gains are not automatic: competition can displace less efficient firms, tariff revenue may fall, and governments need policies that support adjustment and productive investment.

9.What trade theories support the recent rise of China and India on the global stage? Explain your views in detail.

Comparative advantage explains specialization in activities with lower opportunity costs. China developed strengths in manufacturing, while India developed important strengths in IT and business services. Both could gain by exporting these outputs and importing products that were relatively more costly to produce domestically.

Heckscher-Ohlin theory connects these patterns to factor endowments, including abundant labour and particular skills. India's English-speaking technical workforce supported internationally traded services; China's labour supply and production capabilities supported manufacturing. Porter's diamond adds supporting industries, infrastructure, domestic rivalry and demand conditions. Language alone does not explain the difference: investment, institutions, industrial networks and government policies also matter.

10.Some believe there is a disconnect between trade theory and trade policy. What rationale could the United States use to support its trade policies? Give specific examples.

Trade theory highlights the overall gains from specialization, but governments also consider how those gains and costs are distributed. The United States could justify managed trade through socioeconomic objectives, such as protecting health, labour standards and the environment, or geopolitical objectives, such as national security and strategic production capacity.

Illustrative examples include requiring imported toys to meet safety standards, restricting goods made with forced labour, or supporting domestic production of defence-critical components. These objectives do not justify every restriction: measures can raise consumer prices, invite retaliation and protect inefficient firms. A sound policy should address a specific problem proportionately and transparently.

11.When would a country such as France use socioeconomic rather than geopolitical reasons to support its trade policy? Can you provide some examples?

France would use a socioeconomic rationale when the main concern is public welfare, employment, health, environmental protection or community livelihoods. Examples include advocating food-safety standards, supporting workers affected by import competition, or protecting environmental quality in agriculture.

A geopolitical rationale concerns strategic or political objectives, such as safeguarding defence capabilities or applying sanctions to influence another government. France also operates within the EU's common commercial policy, so many trade measures are pursued collectively rather than independently. The distinction concerns the policy's primary purpose.

Chapter 3Regional Economic Integration

Lecture guide: Session3 chapter34.pptx, slides 2-16.

1.Will countries be better off under a multilateral economic liberalization regime or under a system of regional economic integration? Why?

In principle, multilateral liberalization offers broader benefits because it opens trade across many countries and reduces discrimination between members. It gives firms access to more efficient suppliers and larger markets.

Regional integration may be easier to negotiate and can achieve deeper cooperation among fewer countries. Its drawback is trade diversion: preferential treatment may shift purchases from an efficient non-member to a less efficient member. I would favour multilateral liberalization as the broader goal, with regional agreements supporting it through open, compatible rules. The slides identify slow progress in multilateral negotiations as a reason for regional agreements.

2.How useful is economic geography in explaining the success of regional economic integration blocs? Give examples.

Economic geography explains how distance, market size, infrastructure and the location of resources influence integration. Nearby economies can trade at lower transport costs, share production networks and serve a larger combined market.

For example, the proximity of the United States and Mexico makes cross-border manufacturing practical. Dense markets and transport links in Europe support regional distribution. In Southeast Asia, maritime connections and production clusters can support regional supply chains, while uneven infrastructure can limit their reach. Geography is useful, but institutions and political cooperation determine whether these advantages are realised.

3.Why are countries pushing to sign regional integration blocs?

Countries join regional blocs to gain preferential market access, attract investment, achieve economies of scale and strengthen their bargaining position. Integration can also improve access to technology, inputs and skills and encourage political cooperation.

Negotiations among a smaller group of countries may progress more easily than worldwide negotiations. However, governments must weigh these benefits against reduced policy independence, adjustment costs for protected industries and possible discrimination against non-members.

4.Although the European Union is the most advanced form of regional integration, it is currently facing a challenging time. What are some of the major challenges facing the EU?

The central challenge is balancing shared rules and institutions with national sovereignty. Members differ in income, productivity, culture and public priorities, making uniform policies difficult to apply.

In the euro area, a shared monetary policy operates alongside largely national fiscal policies, creating tensions when debt levels and economic conditions differ. Other challenges include uneven regional development, migration disagreements and the distribution of integration's costs and benefits. The sovereign debt crisis and Brexit illustrate these tensions historically, but Brexit should not be explained solely as a consequence of public debt.

5.USMCA has improved Mexico-U.S. trade and investment, yet bilateral trade between the United States and China is greater than with Mexico. Why is this true?

The question's comparison is dated. The course explanation is that China's manufacturing scale, supplier networks and historically competitive production costs attracted trade even though Mexico enjoyed proximity and preferential access. A trade agreement does not, by itself, determine which partner has the largest trade volume.

Mexico's advantages include shorter delivery distances and integration with North American production. As a verified counterexample to the question's premise, U.S. Census figures for December 2025 show goods trade of about US$70.5 billion with Mexico, compared with US$29.5 billion with China. These are monthly goods figures, not annual goods-and-services totals. [1]

6.It appears likely that a free trade area in much of Asia is likely by the end of 2030. What are the global implications for such a development?

A large Asian free-trade area could increase intra-regional trade, encourage specialization, attract investment and reduce costs through larger production networks. Consumers could gain more choice, while firms outside the region would face stronger competition and might invest within it to improve access.

Risks include trade diversion, unequal gains and adjustment pressures on less competitive industries. The prediction is partly overtaken by events: the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) began entering into force on 1 January 2022. It illustrates the move towards wider Asian integration, although an FTA is not a single market and does not remove every barrier immediately. [2]

7.Regional integration in Latin America offers tremendous potential. Yet economic achievements are relatively modest so far. Why?

Regional potential can be weakened by political disagreements, policy reversals, economic instability and overlapping trade agreements. When governments change priorities, firms cannot confidently plan long-term investment around promised market access.

Debt problems, weak infrastructure, administrative barriers and protection of domestic industries can further limit implementation. Similar export structures may also restrict opportunities for complementary regional trade. More stable commitments, simpler rules and improved transport links would help convert formal agreements into practical economic integration.

8.Africa is a huge continent with a wealth of scarce mineral resources and low population density in most countries. Although there are several regional integration blocs in Africa, their performance has been mixed at best, why?

Natural resources alone do not guarantee successful integration. Conflict and political instability in some countries, weak institutions, limited transport and electricity infrastructure, and lengthy border procedures can prevent goods from moving efficiently.

Dependence on primary commodities, limited industrial capacity and overlapping regional rules can also reduce gains. Landlocked economies face additional access costs. Outcomes vary considerably across African countries, so a continent-wide explanation should avoid treating them as identical. Reliable infrastructure, peace, effective customs cooperation and credible implementation are central to stronger results.

Chapter 4The International Flow of Funds and Exchange Rates

Lecture guide: Session3 chapter34.pptx, slides 17-32.

1.In the BOP accounts, what are the four basic components of the current account? How can information about the financing of a country’s current account be obtained?

  • Goods or trade balance: goods exports minus goods imports.
  • Services balance: services exports minus services imports, such as tourism and transport.
  • Income balance: primary income received from abroad minus that paid abroad, including investment income and compensation of employees.
  • Net current transfers: secondary income received minus that paid, such as personal transfers and certain aid flows.

To examine how a current-account deficit is financed, consult the financial account, including direct investment, portfolio investment, other investment and reserve movements. The capital account and statistical discrepancies also enter the full balance-of-payments reconciliation. A deficit can be financed by increasing foreign liabilities or reducing foreign assets.

2.What is the difference between independent floating, managed floating, and fixed exchange rate systems? Provide an example of a direct quote of an exchange rate.

SystemHow the exchange rate is determined
Independent floatingMainly by market demand and supply, without an official fixed target.
Managed floatingMarket forces operate, but authorities intervene to influence the currency's value or fluctuations.
Fixed or peggedAuthorities maintain a target value against another currency or currency basket, sometimes within a narrow band.

A direct quotation states domestic currency per unit of foreign currency. From a Singapore perspective, a hypothetical quote of S$1.35 per US$1 is direct. It is an illustration, not a live exchange rate.

3.Briefly describe the international monetary systems implemented under the Bretton Woods, Smithsonian, and Jamaica Agreements.

  • Bretton Woods, 1944: established an adjustable fixed-rate system in which participating currencies were linked to the U.S. dollar, and the dollar was convertible into gold for foreign official holders. It also established the IMF framework.
  • Smithsonian Agreement, 1971: attempted to preserve fixed exchange rates through currency realignment, dollar devaluation and wider permitted exchange-rate bands.
  • Jamaica Agreement, 1976: accepted greater freedom to choose exchange-rate arrangements, including floating rates, and reduced gold's formal monetary role.

Correction: the U.S. closed the gold window in August 1971, not August 1974 as stated in the PDF. [3]

4.How can the Big Mac Index be used to make PPP comparisons between countries? Using such comparisons, what can be learned about the future possible direction of exchange rates between countries?

The Big Mac Index compares the local prices of a broadly similar product to estimate purchasing power parity (PPP). The implied PPP exchange rate is:

PPP rate = Foreign-currency Big Mac price ÷ U.S.-dollar Big Mac price

For example, if a Big Mac costs S$6 in Singapore and US$5 in the United States, its implied PPP rate is S$1.20 per US$1. If the market rate is S$1.35 per US$1, the Singapore dollar appears undervalued by this measure.

PPP suggests possible long-run pressure towards appreciation of an undervalued currency or depreciation of an overvalued one. It is not a reliable short-run forecast because wages, rents, taxes, trade barriers and local demand also affect prices.

5.Assume that the one-year U.S. Treasury bond rate is 3% and the similar European government bond rate is 5%. Comparing these inflation rates, could it be expected that the dollar will appreciate or depreciate against the euro over the next year? If the spot rate is 1.50 dollars per euro, what would be the forward rate?

Using the PDF's approximation, the forward rate is US$1.47 per euro. The question provides interest rates, despite calling them inflation rates.

Approximate forward premium = iUS − iEurope
= 0.03 − 0.05 = −0.02
F ≈ S × (1 − 0.02)
= 1.50 × 0.98 = US$1.47 per euro

The exact covered interest-parity calculation is:

F = S × (1 + iUS) ÷ (1 + iEurope)
= 1.50 × 1.03 ÷ 1.05
= US$1.47143 per euro

Fewer dollars are needed to buy a euro at the forward rate, so the dollar is at a forward premium and the euro at a forward discount. Under the textbook's additional expectations assumption, this implies expected dollar appreciation of approximately 2%. Covered interest parity itself determines a no-arbitrage forward rate; it does not guarantee the future spot rate.

Chapter 5The Cultural Environment

Lecture guide: Session4 chapter5 with additional and better description of cultural dimensions.pptx, slides 4-32.

1.What are the four characteristics of culture that global marketers must recognize?

  1. Culture is learned: people acquire it through upbringing, education and social interaction; it is not genetically inherited.
  2. Culture is relatively enduring: a company cannot easily change a whole society's values or practices.
  3. Cultural elements vary in importance: firms must identify which aspects matter most in each market.
  4. Business must adjust to culture: operations, communication and marketing should respect relevant local expectations.

These are the four points emphasized in the lecture slides.

2.Name four elements of culture and briefly indicate why they are important when marketing products and services internationally.

  • Language: affects product information, branding and advertising. Accurate translation and back-translation can prevent misunderstandings.
  • Religion: influences food preferences, holidays and acceptable products. A food business may need to adapt ingredients and preparation methods.
  • Manners and customs: shape greetings, negotiation, gift-giving and customer interactions.
  • Aesthetics: influences responses to colours, packaging, symbols and music. A design that appeals in one market may convey an unintended meaning elsewhere.

Researching these elements helps firms communicate appropriately and avoid unnecessary offence.

3.How do Gannon’s cultural metaphors differ from how Hofstede uses culture to classify countries?

Gannon uses cultural metaphors, such as French wine or a German symphony, to describe a culture through a familiar activity or image. This produces a connected, qualitative picture of values and behaviour.

Hofstede uses cultural dimensions, such as power distance, individualism and uncertainty avoidance, to compare country-level patterns systematically. Metaphors help explain a culture as a whole, while dimensions help compare particular characteristics across cultures. Neither approach describes every individual: managers should use them as starting points for inquiry rather than stereotypes.

4.What are some important secondary sources that companies can consult to learn about the cultures of other countries?

Useful secondary sources include government country commercial guides, embassy and trade-agency reports, national statistics, academic books and journal articles, chambers of commerce, industry associations and reputable market-research reports.

For example, a commercial guide may explain business etiquette and negotiation practices, while academic research may explain communication or leadership preferences. Firms should compare sources, check publication dates and confirm important assumptions with local staff or customers because broad national descriptions can overlook regional and individual differences.

5.What are some cultural factors that U.S. companies must recognize when doing business in Latin America?

The slides emphasize relationship-building, personal trust and potentially more flexible approaches to scheduling in parts of Latin America. U.S. managers should allow time to develop rapport, understand local communication styles and clarify expectations for meetings and deadlines.

Respect for titles, hierarchy, family relationships and local language may also matter depending on the setting. Latin America contains many different countries and business cultures. The slide's suggestion that people are less immersed in work should not be treated as a factual judgement about individuals' work ethic.

6.What types of managerial decisions in foreign countries are driven by cultural factors?

Cultural factors affect leadership style, delegation, teamwork, motivation, recruitment, promotion and performance feedback. For example, a manager may need to adapt how openly criticism is expressed or whether rewards emphasise individual or team achievement.

They also affect product design, brand names, packaging, advertising, negotiation and customer service. A hospitality business might adapt food choices, communication and holiday staffing to its market. Managers should combine cultural understanding with individual preferences, local evidence and consistent ethical standards.

Chapter 6The Legal and Political Environment of Global Business

Lecture guide: Session5 chapter6.pptx, slides 4-19.

1.As it becomes more “open” to the world, and more economically developed, how might you expect China’s political system to potentially develop over the next few decades?

Economic openness does not automatically produce political liberalization. One possible path is gradual institutional reform as development increases demand for predictable regulation, accountability and protection of business interests.

Another is continued strong political control alongside market-oriented economic activity. A third is selective reform in areas that support growth while restrictions remain elsewhere. My view is that the safest conclusion is conditional: economic development may create pressure for change, but political outcomes depend on leadership choices, institutions and social conditions. The slides' distinction between economic ideology and political systems helps explain why market reform need not imply democratization.

2.Should the United States Government play a greater role in providing an economic “safety net” for its citizens than it currently does? Alternatively, should such a greater role be the obligation of philanthropic and religious/church groups?

In my view, government should provide a dependable basic safety net because taxation and public institutions can support broad, consistent coverage. Health care access, unemployment assistance and support for vulnerable households can prevent temporary difficulties from becoming long-term poverty.

Philanthropic and religious organisations can complement this through local knowledge, counselling and emergency support, but donations may be uneven and cannot guarantee universal coverage. Programme design should balance adequate support, fiscal sustainability and incentives to work, using clear eligibility rules and employment assistance where appropriate.

3.Why might an international corporation open operations in highly corrupt and politically risky countries, even when it’s well aware of such risks/corruption?

A corporation may accept high political risk because a country offers valuable resources, a large underserved market, low production costs or an early-entry advantage. Management may judge that expected returns justify the risks.

It can reduce exposure through careful partner checks, staged investment, diversification, contractual protections and political-risk insurance. Insurance covers specified events and does not eliminate every loss. Corruption remains a cost and ethical risk; weak enforcement or local expectations do not make bribery an acceptable business strategy. Entry should depend on the ability to operate responsibly as well as profitably.

4.Some observers have argued that the nine Justices of the U.S. Supreme Court have too much power. How does the “common law” natures of the U.S. legal system contribute to this possibility, and how might this situation be potentially reformed?

In a common-law system, judicial decisions and precedent help develop legal rules. Courts therefore influence how broad or ambiguous provisions apply to new situations. The Supreme Court's influence also comes from constitutional interpretation and judicial review, rather than common law alone.

Possible reforms include clearer legislation, stronger transparency and ethics arrangements, and changes to appointment processes. Fixed judicial terms are another debated proposal, but their legal implementation is contested and could require constitutional change. Reform should improve accountability while protecting judicial independence, rather than simply making judges answerable to temporary political preferences.

5.Do you think Google Corporation is a “monopoly” or is violating antitrust “unfair competition” rules/laws? (Google currently has about 89 percent of the U.S. search engine market, with Bing having about six percent, and Yahoo somewhat less.)

A very large market share suggests market power, but does not by itself establish unlawful monopolization. The relevant questions include barriers to entry, the definition of the market and whether the company uses exclusionary conduct to preserve its position.

This question now has a concrete legal development: in August 2024, a U.S. district court found that Google had unlawfully maintained monopolies in general search services and general search text advertising. A September 2025 remedies decision addressed exclusionary distribution arrangements and access for competitors. These are dated court outcomes, not a statement that every Google activity is unlawful or that all litigation has ended. [4]

6.Do you agree with the waiver of patent protections for COVID-19 vaccines? Why or why not?

I would support a targeted, temporary waiver during an exceptional public-health emergency if patent restrictions materially obstruct access to vaccines. Wider production could improve access and reduce avoidable harm.

However, patents also support innovation by allowing inventors to recover research costs. A waiver alone cannot create manufacturing expertise, regulatory capacity or essential inputs. It should therefore be accompanied by technology transfer, training, finance and quality controls, with clear limits and appropriate compensation where feasible. The ethical task is to balance urgent public benefit with incentives for future research.

Chapter 7Corruption and Ethics in Global Business

Lecture guide: Session6 chapter7.pptx, slides 3-15.

1.What do you consider the key characteristics of a person of integrity?

A person of integrity knows what is morally right and has the courage to do it, including when it is inconvenient or personally costly. Important qualities are honesty, consistency, accountability, fairness and respect for others.

For example, an employee who reports an accounting error rather than hiding it to protect a performance bonus demonstrates integrity. Integrity also involves admitting mistakes and applying the same principles when nobody is watching.

2.Why is the ethical character of people so important to business and society?

Business depends on trust, fair dealings and honest communication. Customers need confidence in products, employees need fair treatment, and investors need reliable financial information.

Ethical conduct reduces the need for constant monitoring and costly disputes, supports cooperation and encourages investment. Dishonesty and corruption damage confidence, waste resources and can harm people beyond the company. Ethical character therefore supports both effective markets and social well-being.

3.How does corporate social responsibility contribute to successful business operations?

Corporate social responsibility (CSR) means considering the welfare of people and places affected by business activities. It includes quality products, fair treatment of workers and suppliers, environmental responsibility, legal compliance and appropriate returns to investors.

Responsible conduct can strengthen reputation, customer loyalty, employee commitment and community acceptance while reducing waste and avoidable disputes. For example, a hotel that improves energy efficiency and staff welfare may lower operating costs and improve service. CSR supports long-term success when embedded in decisions rather than used only in promotion.

4.Briefly describe the financial scandals at Enron, WorldCom, Vivendi, and Parmalat; what other scandals have happened in recent years or months?

  • Enron: manipulated earnings and used complex transactions and special-purpose entities to conceal debt and losses, giving investors a misleading picture of performance. [5]
  • WorldCom: improperly recorded operating costs as capital expenditure, understating expenses and inflating reported profits. [6]
  • Vivendi: the SEC's case concerned misleading liquidity statements, improper earnings adjustments and undisclosed financial commitments. [7]
  • Parmalat: reported nonexistent cash and securities and understated liabilities; a purported bank confirmation supporting billions in assets was forged. [8]
  • A more recent example, FTX: customer funds were misappropriated and financial information misrepresented. Founder Sam Bankman-Fried was sentenced to 25 years in prison in March 2024. [9]

These cases demonstrate the importance of truthful disclosure, independent oversight and effective internal controls.

5.How can ethics be taught?

Ethics can be taught through case studies, realistic dilemmas, role-play and discussion of stakeholder consequences. The slides recommend identifying the facts, the people affected and their rights, the available alternatives, and the likely consequences before deciding what is right.

Training should also examine duties, consistency, respect and the wider public good. Ethical leadership, credible reporting channels and rewards that do not encourage misconduct help turn knowledge into behaviour. Training alone will have limited effect if managers reward unethical results.

6.Why are internal controls important to successful company operations?

Internal controls help protect assets, ensure accurate records, prevent and detect errors or fraud, and support reliable decisions. They are especially important when owners rely on managers and managers rely on information from many employees.

Examples include separating payment approval from payment processing, reconciling bank accounts, restricting access to systems, documenting transactions and conducting independent reviews. Controls also help identify problems early so they can be corrected. They reduce risk but cannot completely eliminate collusion or deliberate management override.

Chapter 8Entry Strategies in Global Business

Lecture guide: Session7 chapter8.pptx, slides 3-21.

1.It is anticipated that emerging-market multinationals will play a greater role in international business during this century. What mode of international business operations do you think they would choose? Why?

Well-resourced emerging-market multinationals may favour cross-border mergers and acquisitions. Buying an established business provides faster access to customers, brands, technology, skilled employees and distribution networks than building everything from the beginning.

However, this choice carries integration, valuation and cultural risks. Smaller or less experienced firms may prefer exporting, licensing or franchising, while joint ventures can share risk and provide local knowledge. The best entry mode depends on the firm's resources, desired control, objectives and willingness to accept risk; acquisition is not automatically the best option for every firm.

2.What is a growing development in the evolution of multinational enterprises and how does that development affect globalization?

The slides identify the rise of multinational enterprises originating in emerging economies as an important development. International investment is therefore not simply a flow from developed countries into developing countries.

These businesses create additional sources of capital, technology, competition and management expertise and strengthen links between emerging markets. This can spread economic opportunity and contribute to a more multipolar global economy. However, more international investment does not automatically eliminate development gaps: benefits depend on local skills, institutions and the quality of investment.

3.MNEs try to maximize profits. How could MNEs maximize profits and minimize profit volatility at the same time?

MNEs can combine revenue growth, cost efficiency and geographic diversification. They can enter attractive markets, use economies of scale and locate activities where resources are used efficiently.

To reduce profit volatility, they should select countries and projects whose cash flows are not highly positively correlated. Weak demand in one market may then be partly offset by stronger demand elsewhere. Currency hedging and a mix of suppliers can reduce additional risks. Diversification cannot eliminate global shocks, and its coordination costs must be weighed against the benefits.

4.Quite often, host countries have a love-hate relationship with MNEs. Why?

Host countries welcome MNEs because foreign investment can bring capital, jobs, technology, management skills, competition and tax revenue. Local suppliers may also benefit from new business opportunities.

Concerns arise when powerful foreign firms exploit workers, damage the environment, crowd out local businesses, shift profits abroad or exert political influence. Governments therefore seek the benefits of investment while retaining effective oversight. The relationship improves when MNEs train local workers, develop suppliers, meet environmental and labour standards and engage transparently with communities.

5.To partially overcome its current account deficit, the United States needs to attract FDI that could spur economic growth. What policy measures should the United States take to keep attracting FDI into the country?

The United States could attract productive FDI through predictable regulation, transparent administration, reliable infrastructure, a skilled workforce, protection of property rights and efficient business procedures. Fair competition and a stable legal environment reduce uncertainty for investors.

Investment incentives should be transparent and assessed against the jobs, skills and productive capacity they generate. FDI can help finance a current-account deficit through the financial account, but it does not automatically reduce the deficit itself. Its longer-term effect depends on exports, imports and investment-income flows arising from the new activity.

Chapter 9Control of Global Business

Lecture guide: Session8 chapter9 [Auto-saved].pptx, slides 4-14.

1.In what ways are corporate mission statements more than just “public relations”?

A mission statement explains why the company exists and what it aims to accomplish. It guides strategy, resource allocation, employee decisions and the assessment of performance.

It can also clarify whether the firm prioritises shareholder returns alone or considers a wider range of stakeholders. For example, a hotel mission centred on dependable guest service should influence recruitment, training and service standards. It becomes more than public relations when managers use it to set objectives and are held accountable for following it.

2.Do you think global businesses should place more emphasis upon operational or tactical plans? Why?

Global businesses need both tactical and operational plans. In the slides, tactical plans normally cover one to three years and translate strategy into major programmes. Operational plans cover less than one year and direct day-to-day implementation.

A company entering a new country may initially place more emphasis on tactical decisions about partners, capacity and staffing. An established operation facing service failures may prioritise operational improvements. I would link the two: tactical plans determine what needs to change, while operational plans specify responsibilities, resources and immediate actions.

3.In what positive and negative ways has the Internet changed the conduct and coordination of global business?

The Internet improves global coordination through rapid communication, shared information, online collaboration and real-time performance reporting. It also helps firms reach customers and suppliers across borders at lower cost.

Negative effects include cybersecurity threats, information overload, weaker personal relationships and pressure for constant availability. Headquarters can also monitor subsidiaries so closely that local managers lose useful discretion. Firms should combine common information systems with clear decision rights, appropriate security and opportunities for relationship-building.

4.Do you think six sigma quality control initiatives really work?

Yes, Six Sigma can improve quality when it uses reliable data to identify variation, analyse root causes and improve processes. Its value lies in systematic improvement, not a promise that every operation will become perfect.

For example, a hotel could measure billing errors, identify where incorrect charges arise, improve the handover process and monitor whether errors decline. Success depends on leadership support, employee involvement and measures that reflect customer needs. Excessive bureaucracy or a narrow focus on targets can undermine the initiative.

5.In what ways did the organizational culture at Bernard Madoff Investment Services company help lead to Madoff’s success for so many years?

The case describes a culture of strong loyalty, secrecy and reluctance to challenge authority. Such norms can discourage employees from asking questions and make whistleblowing feel like betraying colleagues.

Staff who benefited from the organisation or feared implication in wrongdoing may have been especially reluctant to report concerns. This illustrates how a strong culture can reinforce misconduct when loyalty is placed above integrity. Independent checks, open challenge and protected reporting channels are necessary; cohesion alone does not demonstrate ethical strength.

Chapter 10The Organization of Global Business

Lecture guide: Session9 chapter10.pptx, slides 3-27.

1.Why are global businesses evolving toward stateless corporations?

Global businesses move towards the stateless corporation model because they can locate production, finance, research and management wherever these activities provide the greatest advantage. Access to talent, customers, capital and efficient suppliers may matter more than concentrating everything in one home country.

This creates a globally integrated business whose identity is less tied to one nation. Nevertheless, a completely stateless corporation is an idealised concept: companies still have legal incorporation, tax responsibilities and obligations in the countries where they operate.

2.Organizational structures can help a company fail or succeed. Why do you think that is the case?

Organizational structure determines who performs tasks, who has authority and how activities are coordinated. A suitable structure supports specialization, clear accountability, communication and effective resource use.

A poorly matched structure can produce slow decisions, duplicated work, departmental conflict and unclear responsibilities. For example, excessive centralization may delay responses to local customers, while excessive decentralization may weaken consistency. Success depends on matching structure to strategy, scale, product complexity and the need for local adaptation.

3.What is the difference between export departments and international divisions?

An export department usually manages overseas sales and related export activities while most of the business remains domestically focused. It commonly emerges as export orders grow beyond what a few sales employees can manage.

An international division has broader responsibility for foreign business, potentially including overseas production, marketing, subsidiaries and adaptation to local markets. It concentrates international expertise but can create separation or rivalry between domestic and foreign operations. It therefore needs effective coordination with the rest of the organisation.

4.Compare and contrast the following organizational structures: functional structure, product structure, market structure, and geographical region structure.

StructureBasis of groupingMain benefitMain drawback
FunctionalSimilar expertise, such as finance, marketing or operationsSpecialization, efficiency and economies of scaleDepartmental silos and weak coordination across functions
ProductProduct lines, such as beverages and snacksClear product focus and accountabilityDuplication of functions and competition for resources
MarketCustomer groups, such as consumers, businesses and governmentCloser fit with different customers' needsOverlapping activities and higher coordination costs
GeographicalRegions or countries, such as Asia and EuropeResponsiveness to local conditionsRegional duplication and inconsistent practices

Product, market and geographical structures are forms of divisional structure. Their suitability depends on which differences the company most needs to manage.

5.Which type of companies often adopt hybrid structures?

Large, diversified international companies often use hybrid structures because they must manage several different demands at the same time. No single grouping may adequately support all their activities.

For example, a company could organise major businesses by product but manage sales within each business by geographical region, while keeping some central functions such as treasury. A hybrid structure offers flexibility, but it requires clear reporting relationships to prevent overlap and confusion.

6.A matrix structure may provide the advantages of the functional and divisional structures, but it may also provide the disadvantages of these two structures. Argue in favor of or against this assertion.

I agree. A matrix structure combines functional specialization with a divisional focus, allowing specialists to work across products, projects or regions. It can improve resource sharing, innovation and responsiveness to several demands at once.

However, employees may report to both functional and divisional managers. Conflicting priorities can create role ambiguity, power struggles, slow decisions and additional meetings. The matrix therefore combines potential strengths and weaknesses of both approaches. Clear decision rights, shared objectives and effective conflict resolution are essential for making it work.

Chapter 11Global Human Resource Management

Lecture guide: Session10 chapter1112.pptx, slides 3-20.

1.What are some of the potential problems global businesses face when they outsource or subcontract their manufacturing work to companies in other countries? (e.g., a U.S. sneaker company outsourcing the manufacturing of its sneakers to a separate company in China or Vietnam)?

Global outsourcing can create problems with quality control, delivery reliability and supplier dependence. Language differences, time zones and geographical distance make supervision and coordination harder.

Other risks include labour abuses, unsafe working conditions, intellectual-property leakage, hidden logistics costs and difficulties enforcing contracts. A sneaker brand can suffer reputational damage if its supplier mistreats workers, even though the factory is separately owned. Supplier due diligence, measurable standards, inspections, worker grievance channels and alternative sources help manage these risks.

2.What are the pros and cons of using “Zoom”/the Internet for the “virtual staffing” of corporate operations?

Advantages: virtual staffing provides access to international talent, reduces relocation and travel costs, permits flexible collaboration and can connect teams quickly across countries.

Disadvantages: it is harder to build trust, interpret nonverbal cues, transfer tacit knowledge and understand local conditions. Time-zone differences, connectivity problems and isolation may also reduce effectiveness. Shared communication rules, suitable technology, regular personal contact and occasional in-person meetings can help. Virtual staffing works best when the role does not require continuous physical presence.

3.Why is it often very difficult for supervisors, in any culture, to provide employees with a negative performance appraisal?

Negative appraisals are difficult because supervisors may fear conflict, upsetting employees or damaging relationships. Feedback may also affect pay, promotion and an employee's confidence, making the discussion emotionally sensitive.

Cultural expectations about hierarchy, directness and saving face can increase the difficulty. Managers should use specific evidence, focus on behaviour rather than personality, give feedback privately and allow the employee to respond. Agreeing on practical improvement steps and training makes the appraisal more constructive.

4.Do you think the new USMCA will be more effective in protecting labor rights than NAFTA? If so, why?

USMCA has stronger enforcement tools than NAFTA's labour side-agreement approach. Its labour commitments form part of the main agreement, and its facility-specific rapid-response mechanism provides a route to address certain denials of freedom of association and collective bargaining in covered facilities. [10]

Its automotive labour-value-content provisions also require 40-45% of vehicle content, depending on vehicle type, to meet specified high-wage requirements associated with at least US$16 per hour. This is not a general minimum wage for every worker in the three countries. [11] I would expect stronger protection where these mechanisms are enforced consistently; treaty wording alone cannot guarantee compliance.

5.Disability law statutes throughout the world generally require employers to make “reasonable accommodations” in the workplace for employees that are disabled? In the world of U.S. sports, for example, what types of accommodations do you think professional baseball, football, or basketball teams should be required to make for players that suffer some form of documented disability?

Possible accommodations include accessible training facilities, adapted communication methods, permitted assistive equipment, and scheduling adjustments or leave for treatment. A player with a hearing impairment, for example, might benefit from visual coaching signals.

In the U.S. employment framework, the assessment is individual: a qualified employee must be able to perform the job's essential functions with reasonable accommodation, without imposing undue hardship on the employer. Accommodation does not generally require removing essential functions. [12] For professional sport, the specific role, applicable law, safety evidence and sporting rules all matter; a diagnosis alone does not establish which adjustment is appropriate.

6.What are some ways global authorities may be able to better protect against the use of abusive child labor in countries throughout the world?

Authorities should combine enforcement with prevention: implement minimum-age and hazardous-work protections, strengthen labour inspection, investigate abuses and hold responsible employers accountable.

They should also improve access to education, family income support and decent jobs for adults so families are less dependent on children's earnings. International cooperation and supplier monitoring can improve visibility across borders. When abuse is found, the response should protect children and support their transition into education rather than simply remove household income. ILO guidance emphasises this combination of legal protection, education and social protection. [13]

Chapter 12Global Marketing

Lecture guide: Session10 chapter1112.pptx, slides 25-48.

1.Why do companies need to research their international markets?

Companies research international markets to decide where to enter, whom to target and how to compete. They need information about customer needs, income, population, culture, competitors, distribution, regulations and political risk.

The slides distinguish market potential, the total possible sales for all suppliers, from sales potential, the portion a particular company expects to capture. Research reduces the risk of choosing an unsuitable market or assuming that domestic success will automatically transfer overseas.

2.What are the three major markets that exist in all foreign markets?

  1. Consumer market: individuals and households purchasing for personal use, such as food, clothing and holidays.
  2. Industrial or business market: organisations buying inputs, equipment or services for their operations, such as machinery and consulting.
  3. Government market: public bodies purchasing goods and services, such as roads, medical equipment and public transport.

These markets differ in purchasing criteria, decision processes, order size and procurement procedures.

3.What is the difference between standardization and adaptation?

Standardization uses essentially the same product or marketing approach across markets. It supports consistent branding and economies of scale.

Adaptation changes products or marketing to match local preferences, culture, purchasing power or regulations. It improves local relevance but can increase cost and complexity. For example, a beverage brand might standardize its logo and quality standards while adapting sweetness levels, flavours and promotional language. Combining global consistency with necessary local adaptation is often called glocalization.

4.How can use of the product life cycle assist international marketers?

The product life cycle helps managers adjust marketing as products move through introduction, growth, maturity and decline.

  • Introduction: build awareness, explain benefits and encourage trial.
  • Growth: expand distribution and differentiate from competitors.
  • Maturity: defend market share, improve efficiency and refresh the offering.
  • Decline: consider repositioning, harvesting or withdrawing the product.

A product can be mature in one country but new in another. International marketers should therefore assess each market separately rather than applying one global timetable.

5.What are the advantages of using trade shows to promote products in international markets?

Trade shows bring a business into contact with many potential buyers, agents and distributors in a short period. Visitors often have a specific interest in the product category, making discussions more targeted.

Companies can demonstrate products, let customers try them, collect feedback, identify sales leads and observe competitors. Direct contact also helps build relationships and explain complex products. To convert attendance into sales, firms should qualify leads and follow up promptly after the event.

6.What are some of the disadvantages that can result when companies use channels of distribution to market their products overseas?

Foreign intermediaries may carry competing products and give greater attention to brands offering better margins. The exporting company can lose control over pricing, promotion, customer service and brand presentation.

Distributor margins add cost, while limited access to customer information can weaken market learning. Poor intermediary performance can damage reputation, and dependence on one channel can make replacement difficult. Clear agreements, training, performance monitoring and suitable channel selection reduce these disadvantages.

7.What are the advantages of using freight forwarders?

Freight forwarders coordinate transport and related logistics for international shipments. They can arrange movement to ports, negotiate freight rates, coordinate carriers, organise storage and assist with customs procedures and shipping documentation.

Their specialist knowledge reduces administrative work and the risk of delays or documentation errors. Consolidating shipments may also reduce costs. They are particularly useful for firms without an experienced in-house international logistics team.

8.How can companies competitively set international prices while avoiding allegations of dumping?

Companies should assess production and export costs, competition and demand, while keeping records that explain their pricing. The slides advise avoiding artificially low export prices and considering transport, distribution and selling costs.

Legal clarification: dumping is assessed through a fair comparison of export price with normal value, usually a comparable home-market price, with alternative methods where appropriate. Being cheaper than local competitors does not alone establish dumping, and simply pricing above marginal cost is not a guaranteed defence. Anti-dumping measures also require an investigation establishing injury and a causal link. [14]

9.How should companies deal with international customers who complain?

Companies should acknowledge complaints promptly, listen carefully, explain the next steps and provide a fair remedy appropriate to the problem. Communication should respect the customer's language and cultural expectations.

Empower employees to resolve routine problems within clear limits rather than forcing customers through unnecessary approval stages. Follow up to confirm satisfaction and record recurring complaints so that underlying problems can be corrected. For example, a hotel receptionist could arrange an authorised room change immediately when a room defect cannot be repaired promptly.

Chapter 13Global Operations and Supply-Chain Management

Lecture guide: Session11 chapter13.pptx, slides 3-22.

1.How can global operations management be used as a source of competitive advantage?

Global operations management creates competitive advantage by improving cost, quality, speed, flexibility and reliability. Firms can obtain suitable inputs worldwide, locate activities efficiently and coordinate procurement, production, logistics and research.

Better processes can reduce waste, shorten delivery times and improve consistency. For example, a hotel group can standardize purchasing specifications to maintain quality while comparing suppliers across markets. The strongest advantage comes from managing the total operating system rather than simply choosing the lowest wage location.

2.List the factors that a firm should consider when making an outsourcing decision.

  • Total cost: purchase price, freight, duties, inventory, monitoring and switching costs.
  • Quality and delivery: supplier capability, consistency, capacity and reliability.
  • Strategic importance: whether the activity is a core capability or a critical source of differentiation.
  • Control and intellectual property: the need to protect knowledge and maintain oversight.
  • Risk: political conditions, currency exposure, disruption, contract enforceability and supplier dependence.
  • Ethics and sustainability: labour conditions, environmental practices and reputational implications.
  • Flexibility: the ability to change volume, switch suppliers or bring work back in-house.

Outsourcing is attractive when the benefits exceed these costs and the supplier can reliably meet requirements.

3.Argue why some companies should pursue global production and other companies should not.

Global production suits firms that can gain from international specialization, lower total costs, access to skills or inputs, and large-scale facilities. Products with a high value-to-weight ratio, such as certain electronics, can often be shipped economically from a limited number of locations.

Other firms benefit from local production because outputs are bulky, perishable, highly customized or require immediate contact with customers. Services such as accommodation must largely be delivered where customers use them. Infrastructure, trade barriers, political risk and supply resilience also affect the decision. A company should pursue international production only where its overall advantages exceed the coordination and risk costs.

4.How should a business organization choose a location for its production facilities?

First compare one central facility with several regional facilities, considering fixed costs, minimum efficient scale, expected demand and transport costs. Then evaluate countries and sites for workforce capability, inputs, infrastructure, market access, political stability and total operating costs.

Proximity to customers and suppliers can shorten delivery times. Product adaptation, perishability and the reputation associated with a production location may also matter. Firms should use a weighted comparison and test scenarios such as exchange-rate changes or supply disruption. Low wages alone are an inadequate basis for a long-term location decision.

5.Do you think that supply-chain management and ERP Systems have helped both businesses and consumers? Why or why not?

Yes, when implemented well. Supply-chain management coordinates the flow of products, information and money, while ERP systems integrate business processes around shared data.

Businesses can improve inventory visibility, reduce duplicate data entry and coordinate purchasing, production, sales and finance. Consumers can benefit from more reliable availability, accurate orders and faster delivery. International ERP features can support multiple languages, currencies and accounting requirements. However, implementation is expensive and depends on data quality, staff training and suitable processes; a poorly implemented system can spread errors across the organisation.

Chapter 14Global Financial Management

Lecture guide: Session12 chapter1415.pptx, slides 3-18.

1.What is the difference between foreign exchange risk arising from translation, transactions, and economic risks?

RiskMeaningExample
Transaction riskExchange-rate movements change the domestic-currency value of a contracted foreign-currency receipt or payment.A Singapore importer owes US$100,000; a stronger U.S. dollar increases the Singapore-dollar payment.
Translation riskCurrency conversion changes reported amounts when foreign subsidiaries' accounts are consolidated.A subsidiary's unchanged local-currency assets translate into fewer parent-currency units after depreciation.
Economic riskCurrency movements affect longer-term competitiveness, future operating cash flows and business value.A stronger home currency makes exports more expensive to foreign customers and can reduce future sales.

Translation can change reported figures without an immediate cash movement; transaction and economic exposure directly concern actual or future cash flows.

2.What derivative securities can be used to hedge the effects of fluctuations in currency values on the cash flows of firms and investors?

  • Forwards: customised agreements, usually with a financial institution, to exchange currencies at a fixed future rate.
  • Futures: standardized exchange-traded contracts, normally involving margin and daily settlement.
  • Options: give the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell currency at an agreed rate, in return for a premium.
  • Currency swaps: exchange specified cash flows in different currencies, often including principal and interest.

For example, an importer with a known dollar payment can buy dollars forward to lock in its domestic-currency cost. Instrument choice depends on the amount, timing and certainty of the exposure and the cost of protection.

3.Briefly describe the main sources of finance for international trade and investment. Distinguish between short-term and long-term finance sources.

Short-term finance supports working capital and trade transactions. Sources include bank credit lines, short-term trade loans, supplier credit, receivables financing and bankers' acceptances. Letters of credit support trade by providing conditional bank payment commitments; they can be combined with financing.

Long-term finance supports facilities, equipment and expansion. Sources include retained earnings, long-term bank or syndicated loans, international bond issues and equity issues. Export-credit agencies may provide loans, guarantees or insurance for qualifying transactions. The slides also discuss IMF and World Bank funding, but this should not be treated as ordinary commercial borrowing directly available to every firm.

4.Based on multinational capital budgeting principles, how should a parent firm evaluate investments in its international subsidiaries? How could the cost of capital for discounting purposes be estimated?

The parent should assess a foreign investment using incremental after-tax cash flows that contribute to the group's value, expressed consistently in the parent currency. Include the initial investment, working capital, operating cash flows, terminal value, effects on existing business, taxes, exchange rates and restrictions on remittances. Avoid double-counting payments between the parent and subsidiary.

NPV = − Initial investment + Σ [Expected cash flow at time t ÷ (1 + r)t]

For a standalone project without capital constraints, a positive NPV indicates value creation. A starting discount rate is an appropriately estimated weighted average cost of capital:

WACC = [E ÷ (D + E)] × ke + [D ÷ (D + E)] × kd × (1 − T)

E and D are market values of equity and debt; ke and kd are their required returns; T is the applicable tax rate. Adjust for project and country risk where justified, match currency and inflation assumptions, and avoid counting the same risk in both cash flows and the discount rate.

5.Can changes in exchange rates affect the stock returns of firms? Use stock return models to explain your answer.

Yes. Currency movements can change revenues, input costs and the parent-currency value of foreign earnings, affecting expected cash flows and therefore share prices.

A market model can be extended to include exchange-rate exposure:

Ri,t = αi + βiRm,t + γiΔet + εi,t

Ri,t is the firm's stock return; Rm,t is the market return; Δet is the percentage exchange-rate change; and γi measures currency sensitivity after controlling for the market. If e is domestic currency per foreign currency, a rise means domestic depreciation. A net exporter may have positive γ, while an import-dependent firm may have negative γ.

The sign is not universal because overseas costs, debt, competition and hedging matter. This is consistent with the valuation model P0 = Σ E(CFt)/(1 + r)t: exchange rates can affect cash flows and sometimes required returns. The PDF's 10-20% estimate is a study-specific historical statement, not a universal rule.

Lecture guide: Session12 chapter1415.pptx, slides 24-43.

1.What is the difference between management accounting and financial accounting?

AspectManagement accountingFinancial accounting

For example, a hotel budget is management accounting, while its annual financial statements serve financial accounting purposes.

2.Describe U.S. GAAP and why it differs from GAAP in other countries.

U.S. GAAP means generally accepted accounting principles used in the United States. These principles guide the recognition, measurement, presentation and disclosure of financial information.

National accounting systems developed in different legal, economic and cultural environments. Differences in investor needs, financing systems, taxation and litigation therefore contributed to different practices. The slides describe U.S. GAAP as relatively rules-based and IFRS as relatively principles-based. This is a broad comparison: both contain principles and detailed requirements.

3.What are the benefits of every country using the same GAAP; that is, IFRS? Why haven’t all countries adopted IFRS?

Common standards such as IFRS improve comparability and make it easier for investors to assess firms across borders. Multinationals can reduce the effort of reconciling different reporting systems, while lenders and analysts gain more consistent information.

Adoption can be difficult because countries have existing legal and tax arrangements, institutional preferences and different reporting needs. Businesses also face training, systems and transition costs, and governments may wish to retain standard-setting control. Even identical standards do not guarantee identical reporting quality: enforcement, judgement and audit quality remain important.

4.How does auditing contribute to better financial reporting?

Independent external auditing increases confidence by examining whether financial statements are fairly presented under the applicable reporting framework and are free of material misstatement.

Auditors plan their work, assess risks, obtain and evaluate evidence, and issue an opinion. Procedures may address the existence, completeness, valuation, ownership and presentation of reported items. Audits provide reasonable rather than absolute assurance; they do not guarantee that every error or fraud will be found. Internal audit separately helps assess and improve controls within the organisation.

5.How does financial ratio analysis enable an investor to compare two companies?

Financial ratios express relationships between figures, making it easier to compare companies of different sizes. Useful categories include profitability, liquidity, efficiency and leverage.

For example, Company A with debt of $2 million and assets of $10 million has a debt-to-assets ratio of 20%. Company B with debt of $1 million and assets of $2 million has a ratio of 50%. B has less debt in dollars but greater leverage relative to its assets. Comparisons should consider industry, accounting policies, reporting periods and trends; one ratio rarely provides a complete judgement.

6.Before locating operations in a country, why should a MNC evaluate that country’s tax system?

An MNC must assess taxes because they affect after-tax cash flows, investment returns, operating costs and compliance obligations. Relevant categories may include corporate income tax, withholding taxes, payroll taxes, customs duties, property taxes and VAT or GST.

It should examine how profits can be repatriated, whether double-taxation relief is available and how intercompany transactions are treated. These considerations influence location and financing decisions. However, a low headline tax rate should not outweigh weak infrastructure, an unsuitable workforce or serious political risk; the objective is a viable operation with attractive overall returns.
